Taking notes from a video sounds easy. You press play, you write down what the teacher says, and you end up with a tidy page. Yet many students finish a lesson with three pages of notes and little understanding, or with no notes at all and a vague feeling that it was "interesting". Neither helps you in an exam or at work.

The purpose of notes is not to record a lesson. The video already does that, and you can replay it whenever you like. The purpose of notes is to make you think while you watch, and to give your future self a short, clear way back into the material. This guide shows how to do both, with three proven methods and templates you can start using today.

## What notes are really for

Good notes do three jobs:

1. **They keep your mind active.** Deciding what is worth writing down forces you to process the lesson instead of letting it wash over you.
2. **They create something to review.** A page you can scan in five minutes is far more useful than a one-hour video you would have to watch again.
3. **They show you what you do not understand.** If you cannot explain an idea in a sentence of your own, you have found a gap worth fixing.

Notice what is missing from that list: a perfect transcript. If you write down everything, you are acting as a stenographer, and stenographers are not required to understand what they type.

## Typing or handwriting?

A frequently quoted 2014 study by Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer, "The Pen Is Mightier Than the Keyboard", found that students who took notes by hand did better on conceptual questions than students who typed, partly because typists tended to copy the lecture word for word. Later attempts to repeat the result have had mixed outcomes, so the research is debated and the effect may be smaller than first reported.

The sensible conclusion is not that one tool is magic. It is that whichever you use, you should avoid transcribing and should put ideas into your own words. If you type faster than you think, make a rule to write short phrases, not sentences. If you prefer handwriting, pause the video as often as you need.

## Method 1: Cornell notes

The Cornell method was created in the 1950s by Walter Pauk, an education professor at Cornell University. Its strength is that it builds review into the page itself. Divide a page into three areas:

- **A wide right-hand column for notes.** During the lesson, write key ideas, definitions and examples here, in your own words.
- **A narrow left-hand column for cues.** After the lesson, write a question or keyword for each group of notes, such as "Why does the sign flip?" or "Definition of entropy".
- **A summary strip at the bottom.** In two or three sentences, summarise the whole page.

The cue column is the clever part. To review, cover the notes column, read each cue and try to answer it from memory. That is active recall built into your notes, with no extra effort.

Here is how a Cornell page for a chemistry lesson might look in plain text:

```
CUES                          | NOTES
What is a mole?               | A mole is 6.02 × 10^23 particles (Avogadro's number).
                              | Links grams to number of particles.
How to convert grams to moles?| moles = mass / molar mass  [06:40]
Common mistake?               | Forgetting to use the molar mass of the whole molecule.

SUMMARY: A mole counts particles. To convert, divide mass by molar mass.
```

## Method 2: Outline notes

An outline is a good choice when a lesson is clearly organised, such as a lecture with chapters or a structured course. You write main topics at the left margin and indent supporting points beneath them.

```
1. Photosynthesis
   - Where: chloroplasts
   - Inputs: water, carbon dioxide, light
   - Outputs: glucose, oxygen
2. Light-dependent reactions
   - Happen in the thylakoid membranes
   - Produce ATP and NADPH
3. Calvin cycle
   - Uses ATP and NADPH to build sugar
```

Outlines are quick to write and easy to scan, and they mirror how many teachers structure their lessons. Their weakness is that they can slip into copying. To avoid that, add one line after each section that begins "So what?" and answer it yourself: how does this connect to the rest of the topic?

## Method 3: Question notes

This is the simplest method and works well for beginners. Instead of writing statements, write questions and answer them as you watch.

- Before the lesson, write two or three questions you expect it to answer.
- During the lesson, add new questions as they occur to you, and write the answers when you hear them.
- After the lesson, write one or two questions the lesson did not answer, to look up next.

Question notes lead directly into review. Your notes are already a set of flashcards. They also make you a more active viewer, because you are always listening for the answer to something.

## Method 4: The Feynman check

Physicist Richard Feynman is often credited with a simple test for understanding: explain the idea in plain language, as if to a beginner. You can use this at the end of any note-taking session.

1. Pick one concept from the lesson.
2. Explain it in a few sentences without jargon, from memory.
3. Whenever you hesitate or fall back on a technical term you cannot define, mark it as a gap.
4. Return to the lesson or your notes, fill the gap and try again.

If you can explain it simply, you understand it. If you cannot, you have just spared yourself a nasty surprise in the exam.

## Use timestamps as bookmarks

One of the great advantages of video is that you can jump back to exact moments. Make the most of it by adding a timestamp beside anything you might want to revisit: a hard derivation, a diagram, a key definition or a moment where you were confused.

```
[12:35] Why the integral is split here (re-watch!)
[27:10] Definition of eigenvector (key)
[41:02] Exam tip: units must match
```

Later, instead of searching through an hour of video, you go straight to the right second. ## A routine for each lesson

Here is a note-taking routine you can follow with any of the methods above:

**Before (2 minutes).** Look at the title and chapters. Write two or three questions. Decide which method you will use.

**During (the lesson).** Pause often. Write short phrases in your own words. Add timestamps for anything you want to revisit. Leave space: you will add to the notes afterwards.

**Right after (5 minutes).** Fill the gaps while the lesson is fresh. Write cues or questions. Add a two-sentence summary.

**Next day (5 minutes).** Cover your notes and answer your cues from memory. Mark anything you missed.

**Weekly (15 minutes).** Review the whole set of notes for the week. Cross out what you now find obvious, and highlight what still trips you up.

## Make your notes easy to find later

Notes you cannot find are notes you will not use. Keep a simple structure:

- **One note per lesson,** with the lesson title and date at the top.
- **A short title and a one-line topic summary.** Future you will appreciate it.
- **Consistent tags or headings,** such as "Definition", "Example", "Exam tip" and "Question".
- **A course-level note,** where you keep a running list of open questions and big ideas.
- **A backup.** Download your notes now and then. On Toppertube your notes are saved in your own browser, so use the download buttons to keep a copy, and upload a file later to carry on where you stopped.

## Common mistakes

- **Transcribing.** If you are writing full sentences at lecture speed, you are not thinking.
- **Highlighting instead of writing.** Highlighting feels productive, but it asks very little of your memory. Write a question instead.
- **Never looking at the notes again.** Notes you do not review are a record, not a study tool. Put review dates in your calendar.
- **Copying the slides.** If the information is already on screen or in the description, add what is not there: your questions, your examples and your connections.
- **Making notes too pretty.** Colour coding and decoration can become a form of procrastination. Clear and quick beats beautiful.
- **Skipping the summary.** Writing two sentences at the end is one of the most useful minutes of the whole session.

## Choosing a method

If you are unsure where to start, use this guide:

| Situation | Best method |
| --- | --- |
| A structured lecture with chapters | Outline notes, with a "so what?" line per section |
| Subjects you will be tested on | Cornell notes, so review is built in |
| A topic you are new to | Question notes |
| Any concept you think you understand | The Feynman check as a final step |

You can also combine them. Many students use outline notes during the lesson and add a Cornell-style cue column afterwards.

## Try it on your next lesson

Pick one video you plan to watch this week. Before you press play, write two questions. While you watch, write short notes in your own words, with timestamps for anything difficult. When it ends, close the video, write a two-sentence summary from memory and add three cues. The next day, cover your notes and answer the cues.

That is about fifteen extra minutes, and it will do more for your learning than watching the next three videos without a pen in your hand.
