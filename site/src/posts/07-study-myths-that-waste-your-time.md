Students are told many things about how to study: find your learning style, highlight the important parts, listen to music while you work, cram the night before. Most of these ideas are widely believed and passed from teacher to student and friend to friend. Some of them are harmless. Others quietly waste hours of effort and leave you less prepared than you think.

This article goes through some of the most common study myths, explains what research says, and suggests what to do instead. The goal is not to make you feel bad about what you have done so far. It is to help you spend the same time and energy far more effectively.

## Myth 1: "I am a visual (or auditory, or kinesthetic) learner"

The idea of learning styles is hugely popular: people supposedly learn best when information is presented in their preferred style, such as visual, auditory or hands-on. Surveys of teachers in many countries have found that large numbers believe it.

The difficulty is that experiments have not supported the key claim. In 2008, a review by Harold Pashler and colleagues looked for evidence that matching teaching to a student's preferred style improves learning. They found very little reliable evidence for it, and concluded that the studies needed to support it were largely missing. Later reviews have reached similar conclusions.

Preferences are real, of course. Many people enjoy one format more than another. But enjoying a format is not the same as learning better from it. What matters is matching the method to the **content**. Geometry is best learned with diagrams, pronunciation by listening and speaking, and a chemistry practical by doing it. Most subjects benefit from several formats, so combining them is a good idea for everyone.

**Instead:** Use the format that fits the material, and use more than one. Watch a lesson, sketch a diagram, explain it aloud and solve problems.

## Myth 2: "Highlighting and underlining help me remember"

Highlighting feels productive. The page fills with colour and you feel you have found "what matters". But research suggests it adds little to memory. In a large 2013 review of study techniques, John Dunlosky and colleagues rated highlighting and underlining as having low utility, partly because they usually involve little thinking. Many students highlight too much, and the act itself does not make you retrieve or connect anything.

**Instead:** Use highlighting sparingly, as a first pass to mark candidates, and then do the real work: close the book and write a summary from memory, or turn the highlighted points into questions you can answer.

## Myth 3: "Re-reading my notes is a good way to revise"

Re-reading is the most common revision method, and it makes sense why: it is easy and it feels as if you are strengthening your memory. The material feels familiar, and familiarity is easily mistaken for knowledge. This is sometimes called the fluency illusion.

The problem is that recognising something on the page is much easier than recalling it unaided in an exam. Studies on the testing effect, including a well-known 2006 experiment by Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke, found that students who practised recalling material remembered it far better a week later than students who simply re-read it, even though the re-readers felt more confident.

**Instead:** Turn re-reading into recall. Read a section, close the book, write what you remember, and then check. Use question cards or past papers. If you do re-read, do it after you have tried recalling, to fix the gaps.

## Myth 4: "Cramming works if I am under pressure"

Cramming can work for a test tomorrow. Intense, last-minute study does produce short-term results, and that is why so many people rely on it. But the information fades quickly, and it rarely forms a lasting foundation. If you are building on the topic later, such as in a later course or a cumulative exam, you will need to learn it again.

The reverse of cramming is spaced practice, in which the same total amount of study is spread across days and weeks. Spaced practice is one of the most reliable findings in memory research, and it leads to much better long-term retention.

**Instead:** Start earlier, even with short sessions. A little each day, with reviews after a day, a week and a month, beats one long session. Our guide on [active recall and spaced repetition](/blog/active-recall-and-spaced-repetition) shows how to do it.

## Myth 5: "I can multitask while studying"

Many students study with a phone beside them, a chat window open and a video playing. It feels efficient, but the brain does not truly do two demanding things at once. It switches between them, and each switch has a cost. Research on media multitasking and learning has found that students who split their attention while studying tend to learn less and take longer, and that even short interruptions can disrupt complex thinking.

Notifications are particularly damaging, because even glancing at one pulls your attention away and takes time to rebuild.

**Instead:** Study in focused blocks of 25 to 50 minutes with your phone out of reach. Close unrelated tabs. Use a timer, and reward yourself with a short break at the end.

## Myth 6: "Background music always helps"

Some people swear by music while studying, others by silence. The evidence is mixed. Music with lyrics tends to interfere with reading and writing tasks, because the brain processes the words in both places. Instrumental or ambient music may help some people, particularly for simple, repetitive work, by masking distracting noise, but it can also reduce performance on demanding tasks for others.

**Instead:** Experiment honestly. For difficult reading or problem solving, try silence or very quiet instrumental sound and compare how much you actually completed. If you notice yourself drifting, turn it off.

## Myth 7: "Smart people do not need to study much"

Many students believe that ability is fixed, so struggling means you are not smart. This belief makes students avoid difficult tasks and give up quickly. In reality, difficulty is often a sign that learning is taking place. Psychologist Robert Bjork has used the term "desirable difficulties" for conditions that make learning feel harder in the moment, such as spacing, testing and mixing topics, but lead to better long-term learning.

**Instead:** Treat struggle as normal. When something feels hard, slow down, break it into smaller steps and ask for help, rather than assuming you are not capable.

## Myth 8: "Longer study sessions mean better results"

Hours spent at a desk are not the same as hours of learning. After a certain point, concentration drops, and the extra time produces little. A shorter, focused session with active recall often beats a long, distracted one.

**Instead:** Measure what you accomplish, not how long you sat. Set an outcome for each session, such as "solve 15 problems" or "explain chapter 4 without notes", and stop when you reach it, or take a break when your focus fades.

## Myth 9: "Watching more videos means learning more"

This one is close to home. Videos are an excellent way to learn, but watching many in a row is passive. The explanation seems clear, so you feel you understand, yet you have not yet shown that you can do it yourself. A playlist finished in a weekend is often forgotten in a week.

**Instead:** After each lesson, close the video and write down what you remember. Take notes with timestamps so you can revisit hard parts. Then practise with problems or a small project. ## Myth 10: "Last-minute all-nighters are a badge of honour"

Many students sacrifice sleep to study. But sleep supports memory consolidation, attention and mood. Tired students concentrate and reason less well, and an all-nighter before an exam can leave you worse off than a few extra hours of rest would have.

**Instead:** Protect your sleep, especially in the days before an exam. Stop new study early the night before, review light summaries and go to bed on time.

## What actually works

If the myths are off the table, what is left? Much of the research points to a small set of habits:

1. **Practice testing.** Recall instead of re-reading.
2. **Spaced practice.** Spread study over days and weeks.
3. **Interleaving.** Mix different problem types in one session.
4. **Elaboration.** Ask "why?" and "how?" and connect ideas to what you know.
5. **Feedback.** Check your answers and learn from mistakes.
6. **Rest.** Sleep, breaks and a sustainable routine.

None of these needs special equipment. They simply ask your brain to work a little harder in the right way.

## A quick self-audit

Think about your last week of studying and ask:

- How much time did I spend recalling instead of re-reading?
- Did I spread any topic over several days?
- Was my phone out of reach for at least one session?
- Did I test myself with questions or past papers?
- Did I sleep enough?

Pick one answer you would like to improve, and change just that this week. Small, evidence-based changes add up quickly.
