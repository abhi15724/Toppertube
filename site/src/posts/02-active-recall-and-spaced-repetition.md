Everyone has had this experience. You spend an evening studying, feel confident, and a week later the material has mostly vanished. It is tempting to conclude that you have a bad memory. In reality your memory is working exactly as designed: it throws away information that you do not use. The good news is that research on learning has identified two techniques that tell your brain, very effectively, that something is worth keeping. They are called active recall and spaced repetition, and together they are the closest thing study science has to a cheat code.

## Why we forget

In the 1880s the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus tested his own memory by learning lists of nonsense syllables and checking how many he could recall after different delays. He found that forgetting is fastest right after learning and then slows down, a pattern now known as the forgetting curve. The exact numbers depend on the material and the person, but the shape holds up well: a large part of what you learn is gone within days unless you do something about it.

Two things change that curve. First, retrieving information from memory makes it stronger. Second, doing that retrieval at gradually increasing gaps makes the memory last longer each time. Those two ideas are active recall and spaced repetition.

## What active recall is

Active recall means trying to produce an answer from memory instead of looking at it. Reading a page, watching a video and highlighting a paragraph are all passive: the information is in front of you, and your brain recognises it. Recall is active: you close the book and try to bring the idea back yourself.

A famous 2006 study by Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke compared students who re-read a text with students who spent the same time trying to recall it. A few minutes later the re-readers felt more confident, but a week later the students who had practised recall remembered far more. This is known as the testing effect. A major 2013 review of ten popular study techniques by John Dunlosky and colleagues rated practice testing as one of the most useful, while highlighting and re-reading ranked low.

The reason is simple. The effort of pulling something out of memory, especially when it is a little difficult, strengthens the pathway to it. Easy, comfortable review feels good but builds little.

### Simple ways to practise active recall

- **Blank-page recall.** After a lesson, close everything and write down all you remember. Then check your notes and add what you missed in a different colour.
- **Question cards.** Turn each important fact or idea into a question on one side of a card, with the answer on the back.
- **Teach it aloud.** Explain the topic to an imaginary student without notes. Wherever you stumble, you have found a gap.
- **Practice problems.** In maths, science and programming, solving problems from scratch is recall in its most useful form.
- **Past papers.** For exams, attempting questions under realistic conditions is both recall and rehearsal.

## What spaced repetition is

Spaced repetition means reviewing material at increasing intervals instead of all at once. Studying the same topic for three hours in one evening, often called cramming, can help you for tomorrow's test, but the memory fades quickly. Spreading the same three hours across several weeks produces much longer-lasting learning. This is called the spacing effect, and it is one of the oldest and most consistent findings in the psychology of memory.

The best moment to review something is just as you are about to forget it. If you review too early, it is too easy and you gain little. If you review too late, you have to relearn it from scratch. A practical compromise is to review at expanding gaps, such as:

| Review | When |
| --- | --- |
| First | The next day |
| Second | After 3 days |
| Third | After 1 week |
| Fourth | After 2–3 weeks |
| Fifth | After 1–2 months |

If you remember an item easily, push its next review further out. If you forget it, bring it back to a shorter gap. Over a few weeks, the material you know well stops demanding your time, and your effort naturally goes where it is needed.

## Putting the two together

Active recall tells you how to review. Spaced repetition tells you when. Combining them gives you a simple loop:

1. Learn something from a lesson, a book or a class.
2. The same day, write a few questions about it.
3. Answer those questions from memory the next day.
4. Answer them again after a few days, then a week, then a few weeks.
5. Move easy questions to longer gaps and bring forgotten ones back.

You can run this loop entirely on paper, or with free apps such as Anki, which schedule reviews for you. The tool matters much less than the habit of testing yourself on a schedule.

## How to write good question cards

Many people try flashcards and give up because their cards are poor. A few rules make a big difference. They come from the "minimum information principle" described by Piotr Wozniak, the creator of the SuperMemo software.

- **One idea per card.** A card with a whole paragraph on the back is a note, not a question. Split it.
- **Ask something specific.** "Photosynthesis?" is weak. "What are the two inputs and two outputs of photosynthesis?" is clear and checkable.
- **Use your own words.** Copying a textbook sentence encourages recognition instead of understanding.
- **Prefer "why" and "how" questions** over pure definitions, so you remember reasoning and not only labels.
- **Add an example.** A short, concrete example on the back makes an abstract idea easier to bring back.
- **Avoid trick questions.** The goal is to strengthen memory, not to catch yourself out.

## A cheap, paper-based system: the Leitner box

If you prefer cards on paper, a method devised by the German science journalist Sebastian Leitner works well. You need a few boxes (or just dividers in a shoebox) and your question cards.

1. Start with all new cards in Box 1.
2. Review Box 1 every day. If you answer a card correctly, move it to Box 2. If you get it wrong, it stays in Box 1.
3. Review Box 2 every three days. Correct answers move to Box 3. Wrong answers go back to Box 1.
4. Review Box 3 every week, Box 4 every two weeks. Again, correct answers move forward and wrong answers go back to Box 1.

Cards you know well drift to the later boxes and appear rarely, while hard cards come back often. It is a spaced repetition system you can build in ten minutes.

## Using these techniques with video lessons

Video is the easiest medium to consume passively, so it benefits most from these techniques. Here is a routine that works with any playlist:

- **During the lesson:** write short notes and add a timestamp next to anything important. On Toppertube you can add timestamps in your notes with one click, and your notes stay beside the video.
- **Straight after:** close the video and write down what you remember. Then turn three to five key ideas into questions, and write them at the bottom of your notes.
- **The next day:** answer those questions without looking. Mark the ones you got wrong.
- **Later in the week:** answer them again. Use the timestamps to re-watch only the sections you missed instead of the whole lesson.

If you turn a playlist into a course, you also get a clear list of what you have finished. That makes it easy to schedule reviews: each completed lesson gets a review date on your calendar.

## Mix topics: interleaving

Another technique pairs well with the two above. Instead of practising one type of problem repeatedly, mix different types in the same session. This is called interleaving. It feels harder and slower while you do it, and your practice scores may even look worse at first, yet studies on maths and other skills have found that it improves performance later, because you learn to choose the right method as well as to carry it out.

A simple way to apply it: in a revision session, do two or three problems from topic A, then topic B, then topic C, and return to A. Do not worry if it feels messy. The extra effort is part of the benefit.

## Common mistakes

- **Re-reading as revision.** It feels safe and familiar, which is exactly why it is weak.
- **Cards that are too big or vague.** If you cannot answer a card in a few seconds, split or sharpen it.
- **Never reviewing.** Creating cards is not learning. The reviews are the learning.
- **Reviewing only what you already know.** Easy cards feel pleasant, but hard cards are where growth happens.
- **Skipping days and then quitting.** If you fall behind, do not try to catch up on everything. Review the oldest cards first, do a manageable amount and carry on.
- **Treating recall as a test of self-worth.** Forgetting during practice is useful: it shows you what to strengthen.

## A 14-day starter plan

If you are new to these methods, try this for two weeks with one subject:

- **Days 1–3:** After every study session, write the blank-page recall and three questions. No cards yet, just the habit.
- **Days 4–7:** Turn your questions into cards or a simple list. Answer yesterday's questions each morning.
- **Days 8–10:** Add a second review: questions from three days ago. Keep each review to ten minutes.
- **Days 11–14:** Add a weekly review of everything so far. Notice which items keep coming back wrong, and rework those cards or re-watch the lesson sections behind them.

By the end of two weeks you will have a working system, and you will probably be surprised by how much you remember.

## The bottom line

You do not remember what you read, watch or highlight. You remember what you retrieve, and you remember it longer when the retrieval is spread out over time. Test yourself early, test yourself often and give your memory a little time to forget in between. A few minutes of honest recall each day will do more for your grades and your skills than many hours of re-reading.
